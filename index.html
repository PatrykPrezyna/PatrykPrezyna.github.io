bottelpack bp 700 Series · Blow-Fill-Seal

The bottelpack bp 700 Series is Rommelag’s Blow-Fill-Seal platform for large-volume parenterals. One machine forms, fills, and seals bottles from 100 to 1,000 ml. I worked this product line from the automation through the packaging equipment: the devices that produce the container, and the molding tools that travel with each format.

The series is built to raise output and shrink the cost around the machine. A bp 730 nearly doubles bottles per hour versus the bp 321 at 1,000 ml. A format change is under two hours. Only 20% of the aseptic cabinet sits in the classified area, so the line fits a 3.5 m room. Cleaning, recipes, and the guided operator interface are part of the same system, on GAMP category 4 software.

Fast prototyping · thoracic aortic repair

Repair of the thoracic aorta interrupts blood flow to the spinal cord. The ischemia can injure the cord and leave the patient paraplegic. That risk runs from 6 to 18 percent. Cooling the cord lowers its metabolic demand. The cooling used today is systemic, and systemic hypothermia brings cardiac, renal, and pulmonary complications.

The idea is local hypothermia: cool the cord, and leave the patient at normal body temperature. I lead the fast prototyping. The hardware moves quickly, and the work stays inside FDA design controls, ISO 13485 for the quality system, and ISO 14971 for risk management.

With clinicians, researchers, and the other stakeholders I turn the clinical need into requirements and specifications. I lead the systems engineering, the risk management, and the design controls. I design the preclinical study protocols and the evaluation methods that will show the device is safe and that it performs. Multiphysics models predict how well the cooling works, guide the next design, and produce data intended for a paper.

Robotic assistance · knee reconstruction

Kneematic is a handheld robotic assistant for the cuts in knee reconstruction. The aim is surgeon adoption. Systems in use today are bulky, slow to set up, and costly to learn. The operation stays the same. The device changes form.

With the MIT team I worked the architecture tradespace: how the robot is mounted, how it is powered, and how it knows where the bones are. The recommendation is a wireless handpiece. The surgeon keeps robotic control of position and of the cutting plane. An inertial unit on the knee and on the device provides navigation, with an infrared camera as the backup. No scan is required before the case. The battery is on the tool. The computation is not. The handpiece is sterilizable, with disposable parts, and it takes more than one cutting tool.

Scored on ergonomics and time in the room, that concept rates 0.79 against 0.63 for the system in use today, and the estimated unit cost falls from about $170,000 to about $94,000. The development plan is about 21 months and $2.4 million, with the project risk brought down by a phased plan and the backup navigation path.

AboutIndustry first, then the research

Ten years on the machine, then the question of how a smaller team models one.

I am a systems engineer and researcher in the Master of Science program at MIT, and a teaching assistant for Model-Based Engineering in the Product Development Lifecycle. The research is MBSE adoption for small and medium-sized organizations. The tools are powerful. The rollout designed for a large enterprise usually is not.

Before MIT I worked at Rommelag across automation, software, and a senior engineering role on the human-machine interface for pharmaceutical equipment. That sequence is the point of view: I trust a model that a commissioning engineer can still use, and I am skeptical of one that only looks complete in a conference room.
